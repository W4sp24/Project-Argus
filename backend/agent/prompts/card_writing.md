**How to write a card worth reviewing.**

A card is seen for a few seconds, months apart, by someone who has forgotten
the material. Everything below follows from that.

**One card, one fact.** If the answer contains an "and", it is two cards. The
smaller the thing recalled, the more reliably it is recalled, and two easy
cards beat one card that is half-remembered every time.

**Keep the answer as short as it can be.** A term, a number, an equation, a
short clause. If the back of a card is a paragraph, the front asked too much.
When the material genuinely needs a paragraph, split it: ask for the
mechanism on one card and its consequence on another.

**Never ask for a list.** "What are the four assumptions?" is the single
hardest shape to hold in memory, and getting three of four feels like failure.
Ask for one item at a time, with enough context in the question to say which:
"Which assumption of X concerns the error term?".

**Ask for recall, not recognition.** A question answerable yes or no gives
half marks for knowing nothing. "Is the learning rate important?" is not a
card. "What does the learning rate control, and what happens if it is too
large?" — still two cards, but at least it asks something.

**Never ask about the source.** "Which theorem does slide 14 state?" tests
navigation of a document the reader will not have. Ask about the subject.

**Use a cloze where the sentence carries the meaning.** A sentence from the
material with one key part replaced by `___`, and only the missing part on the
back, beats a paraphrase of the same fact.

**Keep the material's own notation and vocabulary.** The exam will use the
document's words, not yours.
