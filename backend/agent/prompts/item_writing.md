**How to write a question worth sitting.**

The reader is a student preparing for a real exam. Every question here is one
they could meet in it, written the way an examiner writes one.

**Ask about the subject, never about the document.** This is the rule that
matters most. "According to the excerpt…", "Which theorem is listed…", "What
is on slide 20…", "What does the deck cover after the examples?" — all of
these test whether someone can navigate a file they will not have in front of
them. The source is where the answer came from, not what the question is
about. The citation records it; the question must not.

**Put the situation before the question.** The best stems describe a concrete
case and then ask what follows from it:

> A function $f$ is continuous on $[0,2]$, differentiable on $(0,2)$, and
> $f(0)=f(2)=3$. A student concludes $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(0,2)$. Which
> result justifies the conclusion?

rather than "What is Rolle's Theorem?". A case can be reasoned about; a
definition can only be recognised.

**The cover-the-options rule.** A reader who covers the options should be able
to answer the question anyway. If they cannot, the stem is not asking
anything — it is inviting a comparison of four phrases.

**Write distractors a prepared student could pick.** Every wrong option must
be the same *kind* of thing as the right one — another theorem, another
mechanism, another value in the same units — and must be wrong for a reason
worth understanding. An option nobody would choose is not a distractor, it is
a space.

- Keep them the same length and grammatical shape as the key. The longest,
  most qualified option is the most reliable tell a test-wise student reads.
- Never use "all of the above", "none of the above", or "both A and B". They
  are reasoned out rather than known.
- Avoid `always`, `never`, `every`, `none` in a wrong option. They read as
  false without being considered.
- Do not repeat a distinctive word from the stem in the key alone.
- Do not let two options mean the same thing.

**Negative stems, rarely, and shouted.** If you must ask which item does *not*
belong, write NOT in capitals. Unmarked, it is a reading test.

**Spread the answers.** Across a set, the right answer should not sit in the
same position more often than any other.

**Every question needs one defensible answer.** If two options could be argued
for, the question is not finished. Say in the explanation why the key is right
*and* why the nearest distractor is wrong — that sentence is often the most
useful thing on the page.
